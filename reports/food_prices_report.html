When the rains fail, what happens to food prices?

Twenty years of market prices for maize, sorghum and beans, set against Uganda's rainy seasons and the Indian Ocean conditions that drive them. The biggest price risk turns out to be the predictable yearly cycle between harvest and lean season. On top of it, a poor first season adds about 9–11% to maize prices, and the Indian Ocean in July–August hints at the following year's lean-season prices.

Key takeaways

  1. The yearly price cycle is the biggest, most predictable risk. Maize is about 30% dearer in May–July than at harvest. Farmers who sell at harvest and buy back later lose much of that, which is the case for better storage. High
  2. A dry first season raises maize prices. About 9–11% per standard deviation of rainfall shortfall, in two independent price datasets. Medium
  3. A dry second season raises sorghum prices by about 8–9% the next February–May. Suggestive
  4. The July–August Indian Ocean Dipole points to next year's lean-season prices: a strong positive reading has been followed by maize about 10–17% cheaper. Suggestive
  5. Satellite crop greenness confirms the chain from rain to harvest to prices, and picks out the droughts of 2005, 2008–09, 2016–17 and 2021–22. It predicts prices about as well as rainfall, not better. Medium
  6. Karamoja moves with the national maize market but swings harder, with the largest seasonal cycle in its staple and prices near double the norm in mid-2022. High
  7. National crop statistics can't measure weather shocks. FAOSTAT's Uganda series are dominated by method changes and projections. High

Why beans differBeans show no response in either season. Most bean-growing areas plant in both seasons, and beans trade widely across East Africa, so one poor Ugandan season is buffered. Maize and sorghum, grown mainly once a year in much of the north and east, respond.
03 · How sure are we?

The size is stable; the certainty is limited by the number of years

The seven markets move together, so they add little independent evidence: the real sample is 15–16 years. Three stricter checks: a permutation test that compares the result with thousands of reshuffled rainfall histories, re-estimating with each year left out, and an independent sample of wholesale maize prices from 2006–2021.

A puzzleFrom 2016 to 2022, crops in the centre and south were less green than rainfall predicts. Two satellites that cross at different times of day agree, so it is not a sensor fault. Possible causes: fall armyworm, which reached Uganda in 2016; dry spells within months; satellite rainfall overstating those years; and, in Central, a steady drift from using a fixed 2021 cropland map while land was being converted. Greenness predicts prices about as well as rainfall, not better, but it confirms the price effects run through the harvest.
06 · Karamoja

Connected to the national market, but more exposed

Karamoja's real staple prices since late 2018, against their own average. The 2022 spike began early in the year and peaked in June–July, before that year's harvest. Satellite greenness shows Karamoja's own crops and pasture near normal in 2021–22, while the rest of Uganda had some of its worst seasons on record. The spike fits a national shortage reaching Karamoja through the market, on top of the global grain price shock after Russia's invasion of Ukraine and local insecurity.

07 · Crop statistics

Uganda's national crop figures can't show weather shocks

Area harvested as reported to FAOSTAT. A change in method in 2008 moves every crop at once, in opposite directions; in 2013–2015 (shaded) the figures barely move at all, the signature of projections. This is why the analysis uses market prices.

08 · What it means

Implications for farmers, markets and food security

These follow from the price evidence. The storage and early-warning points in particular should be tested with farm-level data before they shape programmes.

Farmers

Storage pays, if losses are low

  • Holding maize from harvest to the lean season earns about 25–30% in real terms on average.
  • Traditional storage often loses 10–20% of grain, which eats most of that gain. Hermetic bags and warehouse receipts are what make storage worthwhile.
  • Prices peak when household stocks are lowest, so selling everything at harvest is the costliest choice.
Markets and policy

Rainfall is a price signal months ahead

  • A poor March–May season signals higher maize prices from September, three to six months ahead.
  • Warehouse-receipt systems and strategic reserves can buy at harvest and release in the lean season, damping the cycle.
  • Because regional harvests rise and fall together, national shortfalls need national responses.
Food security

Karamoja's risk is exposure

  • Its staple swings the most within the year, peaking June–August, when food assistance matters most.
  • Its prices respond to national and global shocks, as in 2022, not only to local rains.
  • A positive July–August IOD is a hopeful sign for next year's prices; a negative one is a reason for early preparedness.

09 · Methods and limits

How this was done

Method

  • WFP monthly retail prices deflated by Uganda's consumer price index, logged, with each market's trend and calendar-month pattern removed
  • Outcome: average price anomaly over September–December (first season) or the next February–May (second season)
  • OLS with market fixed effects and the world maize price; exchange rate as a robustness check; errors clustered by year
  • Permutation tests, leave-one-year-out estimates, and an independent wholesale sample
  • Crop greenness: MODIS NDVI over ESA WorldCover cropland, monthly by region, from Terra with Aqua as a cross-check

Limits

  • 15–16 years of prices; the permutation tests are the honest measure of certainty
  • Retail prices in towns, not farm-gate prices, which are lower and more volatile
  • A national price index for deflating; regional inflation can differ
  • Karamoja prices only from late 2018, too short to estimate rainfall effects there
  • The 2021 cropland map under-counts the banana–coffee belt, so Central and Western greenness covers fewer fields

Next steps. Resolve the 2016–22 greenness puzzle with station rainfall and a year-by-year cropland map; test April–June EVI as an early first-season signal on coming seasons; and add daily rainfall measures (onset, dry spells) that monthly totals miss.
